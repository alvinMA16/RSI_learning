THE COMPLEX OF CURVES PAIRWISE INTERSECTING AT MOST
ONCE IS CONTRACTIBLE IN GENUS TWO
Abstract. Let S2 be the closed orientable surface of genus two and letC1(S2) be the flag
complex on isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves in which a finite set of classes spans
a simplex when the classes pairwise have geometric intersection number at most one. We prove
that C1(S2) is contractible. The proof transports the whole problem to the six-times-marked
sphere through the hyperelliptic involution, for which we give a self-contained proof of the
required dictionary, and then runs a filtration spectral sequence on Hatcher’s contractible arc
complex of the marked sphere. The single arithmetic input is that a genus two surface has
exactly six Weierstrass points; it enters as a budget which forces every filtration stratum to have
a contractible link.
1.Introduction
1.1.The complexes Cd.Let Sg be the closed connected orientable surface of genusg and let
S(Sg)denote the set of isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves inSg;essentialmeans
embedded and not bounding a disc. Writei(·,·)for the geometric intersection number, that is,
the minimum of|a∩b|over transverse representatives of the two classes. For an integerd≥0
the d-curve complexCd(Sg)is the simplicial complex with vertex setS(Sg)in which a finite
subset{γ0,...,γk}spans ak-simplex if and only ifi(γa,γb)≤dfor alla,b. ThusC0(Sg)is the
classical curve complex of Harvey, and eachCd(Sg)is by construction the flag complex on its
own1-skeleton. The inclusions C0(Sg) ↪→C1(Sg) ↪→···are equivariant for the action of the
mapping class group.
The homotopy type ofC0(Sg)is known: it is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres
of dimension2g−2, so it is highly connected but never contractible. Souto [7] observed that
the situation changes as soon as one allows curves to cross: he proved that the inclusion
C0(S)↪→C1(S)is homotopic to a constant map, and asked whetherC1(S)— or, if not, some
Cd(S)— is contractible. That question is the subject of the present paper. It is genuinely a
question about the ambient complex: all three ofC0(Sg), C1(Sg)and Cd(Sg)have the same,
infinite, vertex set, andC1 differs fromC0 only by the additional simplices created by the edges
withi= 1.
Two features ofC1 make it harder to handle thanC0. First, it is not pure: it has maximal
simplices of different dimensions, so no argument by collapsing top cells is available. Second, the
natural sources of contractible models for curve complexes — arc complexes and their relatives
— are complexes ofdisjointobjects, and the passage from “disjoint” to “crossing at most once”
loses exactly the property that makes those models combinatorially transparent.
1.2.Results.Our main theorem answers Souto’s question affirmatively in genus two.
Theorem 1.1.Let S2 be the closed orientable surface of genus two andC1(S2)the flag complex
on isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves, with a simplex for each finite set of classes
pairwise of geometric intersection number at most one. ThenC1(S2)is contractible.

1.3.Method.The proof has four movements.
(i) Transport to the marked sphere.A genus two surface carries a unique hyperelliptic
involutionι, with six fixed points, andιacts trivially on isotopy classes of simple closed curves.
Consequently the whole complexC1(S2)can be read on the quotient orbifoldΣ = (S2,B), where
non-separating curves become essential arcs joining two distinct branch points and separating
curves become circles splittingB as3|3. We prove this dictionary rather than quote it (Section 4);
the proof consists of a lifting lemma for ambient isotopies, a projection lemma forι-invariant
curves, and a descent lemma whose argument goes back to Winarski’s combinatorial proof of the
Birman–Hilden theorem.

1.4.What is not claimed.Three boundaries of the result should be stated explicitly.
First,only g = 2is settled. The identity6 = 2g + 2is a hard input to the budget argument of
Section 9: at genus three there are eight branch points, the inequality above becomes vacuous,
and the argument does not survive. More fundamentally, a general surface of genusg≥3carries
no hyperelliptic involution at all, so the entire transport to a marked sphere is unavailable.The
method gives no information whatsoever aboutC1(Sg)forg≥3.
Second,the punctured versions are not a weaker instance. One hasC1(Σg,1)≃C1(Sg), the
closed complex being a simplicial retract of the punctured one; so a proof for surfaces with
boundary or punctures cannot be leveraged into a proof for the closed case, and conversely
nothing is gained by first passing to a punctured model.
Third,this paper contains no finite truncation computation. All arguments are structural.
We record this because homology computed on a finite pool of vertices, or on a finite quotient
of such a pool, carries no information about the ambient complex: there is no map in either
direction relating the homology of a finite subcomplex spanned by a chosen finite set of curves to
the homology ofC1(S2), and a non-vanishing Betti number of such a truncation is not evidence
against Theorem 1.1.

3.Two hyperbolic structures
The marked sphereΣ = (S2,B)will carry two different hyperbolic structures, and it is essential
to keep their roles apart. Thecone metricof §3.1 has the six marked points as cone points
of angleπ; its virtue is that its pullback toS2 is asmoothclosed hyperbolic metric, so that
preimages of arcs become honest closed geodesics. Thecusped metricof §3.3 has the six marked
points as cusps; its virtue is that arcs themselves — loops included — acquire complete geodesic
representatives realising minimal intersection, simultaneously for an arbitrary family. Neither
metric can do the other’s work; see Remarks 3.7 and 4.3.

11.1.Where the argument stops.It may be worth isolating the three points at which the
genus enters, since each of them blocks a naive extension.
First, the number6 = 2g + 2of branch points is the budget of Lemma 9.15: the inequality
k(g1) +k(g2)≤6−V′≤5is what forces one of the two free gaps to carry one or two marked
points. Forg = 3the same scheme gives eight branch points and a budget of7, and two free
gaps withk= 3andk= 4are no longer excluded, so the good face need not exist.
Second, and more decisively, a closed surface of genusg≥3has in general no hyperelliptic
involution, so there is no quotient marked sphere and the dictionary of Section 4 has no analogue.
The whole method is therefore confined tog= 2; Theorem 1.1 gives no information forg≥3.
Third, passing to a punctured surface is not a weakening of the problem. The inclusion of the
complex of a once-punctured surface into that of the closed surface is a simplicial retraction, so
contractibility of the closed complex implies that of the punctured one and not conversely; an
argument which assumes the closed case in order to derive the punctured case, and then recovers
the closed case from it, is circular.
Remark11.4.No step of this paper computes homology of a finite subcomplex. This is deliberate:
for the complexes considered here there is in general no map, in either direction, between the
homology of a finite pool of vertices, or of a finite quotient poset, and the homology of the
ambient complex, so such a computation could not be evidence for Theorem 1.1. Every argument
above is structural.
